01 — About me / My name

So, what do people call me?

01 / DUC MINH → TON

Once again, hello! My name is TON DUC MINH, but except for my family, nearly nobody knows my real name. But don't get me wrong, okay? Of course they know my name. But in Vietnam, the name Duc Minh, which means “intelligent,” is very popular. Because of that, in my class, from primary school all the way to high school, there would always be like 5 or 6 boys with the same name. So my friends mostly call me by my family name, Ton.

02 / THE FAMILY NAME

About the family name: Ton here is a pretty special family name. I have the same family name as the very first president of Vietnam. I am often jokingly called the great-great-grandchild of Ton Duc Thang, or the 7,749th-generation descendant of Sun Wukong, the Monkey King.

03 / BEAR

Nickname: Like everyone, I have a nickname at home: Bear. My private email even takes the name Tonminhbear@gmail.com. This is because when I was a kid, I was very gentle and well-behaved, unlike the other kids in my family. And I slept a lot, like a hibernating bear. So that name has stuck with me all the way until now.

FRIENDS CALL METon.MY FAMILY CALLS MEBear.

Sometimes, I even forget what my actual name is.

ACADEMIC PROJECT · ENVIRONMENTAL CHEMISTRY

EnviCa

From the To Lich River to a ZnO/g-C3N4 photocatalytic system.

Why I started

After gathering knowledge of theoretical chemistry for nearly 3 years since grade 8, I wanted to test chemistry in daily life. I lived near the To Lich River, which is “famous for” its pollution (according to every Hanoi resident). So I wanted to test some ideas to clean the water on a small scale, with the hope that someday I no longer have to hold my nose every time I walk past it!

Actually, there are a bunch of methods available theoretically, but the biggest problem is that they are not readily applicable as practical solutions. Only under controlled conditions do those methods work effectively. However, I found a niche.

You see, sunlight is everywhere. So why don’t we utilize it to develop a method? Actually, we can, and that is photocatalysis. Let me explain. Photocatalysis is a method that uses sunlight to activate catalysts so that they can work and degrade harmful toxic compounds. And because these catalysts are usually made from materials such as ZnO and TiO2, both of which are used in sunscreen, they can offer a relatively safe option. The cost of these compounds is also affordable.

But there is a limitation. ZnO alone responds primarily to UV light, while UV accounts for only a small fraction of sunlight. That gave me my next question: could I combine ZnO with another semiconductor to improve the photocatalytic system? I therefore turned to g-C3N4, a metal-free semiconductor with a narrower band gap and greater effectiveness in the visible-light region. Combining the two could form a “perfect” solution.

ENVICA — PROJECT PHOTO · envica-01.jpg

Research & Experimenting

First, with the help of my mentor, I had to delve into theoretical class: what it is, how it works, its mechanism, pros and cons, etc. I also had to read prior research to determine the easiest and most economical ways to synthesize ZnO, g-C3N4, and their composite. I ultimately went with using Zn(NO3)2 + NaOH + HMTA to create ZnO. The reason is that, through this method, the Zn(OH)2 formed would not be dissolved by excess NaOH because HMTA helps suppress this process. As for g-C3N4, the synthesis was straightforward: urea, an inexpensive and easily accessible precursor, could be thermally polymerized through calcination to form g-C3N4. This was important to me because I did not want to design a treatment material that worked scientifically but depended on a lavishly expensive synthesis.

Then I mixed them together, ground the mixture, and calcined it in a furnace to make the composite.

EXPERIMENT / LAB PHOTO · envica-02.jpg

Testing, Results & What Comes Next

The last step was to test its ability to degrade dyes. I chose methylene blue, a persistent compound in water, and tartrazine for testing. The experiment culminated in promising results: 74.9% degradation for methylene blue and 94.2% for tartrazine.

The results were promising, but they also exposed several limitations. First, however, my ZnO/g-C3N4 system still performed much better under high-energy light. Second, a beaker containing one model dye is also very different from an actual river containing many other contaminants.

Therefore, I want to improve the catalyst's response under visible light and explore ways of integrating photocatalysis with other treatment technologies to move toward more complex conditions.

I still cannot clean the To Lich River and still have to hold my nose when I walk past it. But now, I see it as a question that chemistry has not completely answered and one that I aspire to keep working on.

3 MIN READ · ACTIVITY 01

Dưỡng Tâm

01 / STORY

Dưỡng Tâm (Unwinding Mind) is a community project that I developed out of a simple desire: to bring emotional value to those in need. In my opinion, volunteering is not only about giving material support; offering companionship and spiritual connection is also necessary. With this belief, I went to Bách Niên Thiên Đức—the first nursing home in Vietnam—and began organizing activities there. Let me explain a little bit about the name. “Nurturing the heart” can be understood as caring for the inner well-being of others. I chose this name because caring for the elderly is not only about taking care of their physical needs, but also about nurturing their sense of connection. At the same time, “Dưỡng Tâm” carries a highly personal meaning for me.

02 / STORY

As a high school student, much of my daily life was spent at my desk, moving from one chemistry problem to another, preparing for exams, and constantly thinking about what I had to accomplish next. I enjoyed learning, but sometimes I realized that there was a whole world beyond my textbooks that I had barely taken the time to explore. Dưỡng Tâm became my way of stepping beyond that familiar world.

03 / STORY

During the activities, we held two seasons at Bách Niên Thiên Đức between 2024 and 2025, with each season lasting approximately three months. 28 elderly residents joined us each weekend. Each session featured a distinctive theme, ranging from music and art to lighthearted games and creative activities, such as Music Bingo, Word Chasing, and Mystery Touches. In addition, we sang alongside the elderly, sat down to listen to their stories, and shared small gifts, from Custas to handmade crafts. Those heartwarming moments not only brought joy and companionship to the residents, but also offered us a welcome respite from the pressures of academic life.

04 / STORY

Consequently, I intend to continue serving as the Head of Content for the project, or contributing behind the scenes, in the following years. We plan to visit other nursing homes and extend these heartwarming and meaningful moments to more elderly communities.

3 MIN READ · ACTIVITY 02

CodeFutureVN

01 / STORY

This is the second leadership activity I want to tell you guys about. In my previous project, I worked with the elderly. So this time, why don’t we switch things up and work with kids? You know, I’ve spent almost twelve years of my life sitting on the student side of the classroom—and that’s not even counting kindergarten yet. Honestly, being a student can feel a little restrictive sometimes. So, for once, I wanted to put myself in a teacher’s shoes and see what it felt like to be the one “controlling” the students. That was how CodeFutureVN began.

02 / STORY

Actually, this project was also created with the support of a CodeFuture project in the United States. I happened to know a friend there, so I asked him to guide me and then, together with my team, created our own “version” in Vietnam. In this project, we wanted to teach Scratch to students, following the way some high-quality schools had already taught this subject in their computer science classes. Therefore, I wanted to bring these programs to some schools in Hanoi that had not had the chance to experience them.

From student
to teacher.
03 / STORY

First, I went to Nam Trung Yen Primary School. We taught a fourth-grade class. This was the first school we had ever taught at, so many things didn’t go as planned. Some of them already knew about Scratch while others didn’t, so it was really challenging to have a lesson plan that fit all of them. Another problem was that because we didn’t expect some students to already know Scratch, we had to push the lesson plan faster for those students, and we ran out of lessons. However, I handled it by assigning one main teacher to teach the whole class and one team member separately for those students. Luckily, we finished the course on time with all the lessons, with the final result being that every student had one product made by themselves.

04 / REFLECTION

When we moved to Ban Mai School, because we had already learned from the previous experience, this time the lesson-plan problem did not happen. It was just that the class was bigger, so managing it was a little more difficult. Still, these experiences were really valuable to me. They helped me realize that to become a teacher, you need many qualities besides professional knowledge and the ability to speak. You also need the ability to understand students, patience, and being witty and funny.

Patience.
Understanding.

05 — Photography Project

Invisible Traces.

Photography, Vietnamese craft villages, and the chemistry quietly embedded in traditional processes.

PHOTO FROM THE PHOTOGRAPHY WORKSHOP · invisible-traces-01.jpg

I. How it began

Besides academic projects, I have another project that I want to share. This project came to me almost by accident, as I had never planned for it beforehand. But first, I want to admit that I have no special talents. Yeah, I know all four swimming strokes, and I spent four years in a swimming club starting in fifth grade, but I have almost no interest in swimming.

Until last summer, I had the chance to join the Ecoventure Team, led by Ms. Thu, her husband Mr. Brett, and Mr. Đức. All of them are professional photographers who organized a summer camp to teach photography. Most notably, they taught us in Hội An, where we practiced multiple techniques, from panning to the rule of thirds, across different places, from Hội An Ancient Town to traditional craft villages and local sites such as the Thanh Hà fish market, Thanh Hà pottery village, and Kim Bồng mat-weaving village.

Their experience and wholehearted dedication, together with those mornings when I had to wake up at 4:00 AM to photograph the sunrise or the early fish market, made the experience utterly fascinating, even when I was exhausted to the bone and struggling to keep my eyes open. I was able to witness a way of life I had never seen in Hanoi, watching fishermen return from a night at sea and haul their catch ashore to sell, or simply standing on the beach as the sun began to rise. Yet what fascinated me most of all was photo storytelling. I found myself completely captivated by the stories evoked by photographs that spoke not a single word, yet somehow carried a soul of their own. It felt as though they magnified my imagination ten thousandfold, and I wanted to be able to do the same. That was when the first spark of this project began to glow in my mind.

WORKSHOP PHOTO · 02
HỘI AN PHOTO · 03

II. Planning Process

At first, I only intended to photograph craft villages in Hanoi and nearby areas such as Bắc Ninh. However, when I visited Đông Hồ painting village in Bắc Ninh, I heard a legend that “the colors here can be eaten.” It turned out that they created those colors from things so deeply familiar in everyday life, such as Japanese pagoda tree flowers and indigo leaves. They are said to be “edible” because these flowers and leaves can be brewed into tea, not because people actually eat them, of course.

From that moment, I began to wonder whether other villages might have similar “elements” hidden within them. And as I researched further, I realized that chemistry had always been quietly concealed behind traditional processes that seemed so simple at first glance, yet held remarkable depth beneath the surface.

That was when my idea of uncovering and analyzing the chemistry within my photographs began to take shape across different craft villages. Yet it would have been such a waste to let these beautiful photographs simply lie forgotten on my camera's memory card. I had once been amazed by the photo storytelling of my teachers. Now, I wanted to be the one who could surprise others with stories of my own.

CRAFT VILLAGE / EXHIBITION PHOTO · 04

III. From photographs to stories

I wanted to bring these photographs to others and share my experiences with the people close to me, so I organized an exhibition at a café. Whenever someone asked me what a particular photograph was about, I would proudly recount the story of how I had taken it, weaving in bits of very simple chemistry along the way. Seeing the surprise in the eyes of those who came to view my photographs, I felt as though, for once, I was truly shining. And so I ended up telling the entire story of my journey four times to four different groups. My throat was completely parched by the end, but I was genuinely happy that I had managed to do something meaningful, especially for someone like me, who had once been considered shy and without any particular artistic talent.

In the future, I still want to continue this journey of discovery and share the beauty and values of Vietnam's traditional craft villages, the experiences I gather along the way, and the chemistry quietly hidden behind them.
